# Peer-Review Packet: *From Noise to Knowing*

Editorial document. It is not part of the published book and is excluded from the site build.

The packet has three parts: a two-page book summary to send with the manuscript, a five-question review form, and five recommended reviewers.

---

## 1. Two-page summary (send with the manuscript)

**From Noise to Knowing: The Discipline of Turning Information into Knowledge**
Pramod Rathod, AI engineer and researcher. 27 chapters in six parts.

**The problem.** Most working people have more information than ever and are no better at knowing things. The book says the gap is a discipline problem, not an access problem. Information becomes knowledge only through deliberate work, and most of us skip that work or do it by habit.

**The argument.** The book proposes a seven-stage pipeline: selection, pre-processing, generation, testing, integration, retention or discard, and correction. It treats each stage as a skill that can be practised and each failure as something that can be named. It is written in the first person by an engineer. Every chapter opens with a story from the author's own work, such as a retrieval system that failed, a classifier that broke in production, a churn model that scored 0.94, a migration that was correct and wrong.

**The structure.** Part I (Chapters 1–3) sets out the problem. Part II (4–6) lays the foundations: what information, knowledge and the knower are. Part III (7–15) walks the pipeline, with Chapter 15 applying it to worked examples. Part IV (16–19) covers the obstacles: cognitive biases, logical fallacies, social and institutional pressure, and the limits of the individual mind. Part V (20–23) covers tools: personal knowledge management, context engineering, AI as a knowledge partner, and data science. Part VI (24–27) covers mastery: expertise, judgment, wisdom, and the discipline as a way of working.

**What the book offers that is its own.** The research it draws on (Kahneman, Tversky, Polanyi, Popper, Ericsson, Klein, Clark and Chalmers, Shannon, Simon) is established. The pipeline is the author's synthesis of it. The book's named formulations are the author's own, and are meant to be quoted and tested:

- *Recall is not selection* (Chapter 8)
- *The test that cannot fail is a mirror* (11)
- *The fallacy in technical clothing* (17)
- *The courage tax* (18)
- *Scaffold debt* and *extension versus substitution* (19)
- *Confident gap-filling* and the *trust calibration matrix* (22)
- *Temporal leakage* (23)
- *The compression insight* (24)
- *Commensurable versus incommensurable values* (25)
- *The confidence–stakes calibration* (26)
- *The wasted-day insight* (27)

The confidence–stakes calibration is the one the author would like the book to be known for beyond the pipeline. It has its own page in the front matter.

**What it does not claim.** The framework does not solve motivation or institutional power. It is individual-first. It assumes a baseline of literacy, time and safety. Its claims about AI are time-bound. The back matter says so in a limitations appendix, and a front-matter evidence note separates established research from the author's synthesis and formulations.

**Features for readers.** Each chapter carries a Beginner / Both / Advanced flag and a Key Terms box that links to the glossary. Twelve chapters end with four reflection questions. The back matter has a glossary, a checklist, a fallacy cheat sheet, further reading, limitations, an index and a closing note. The book is also published as a website.

**What we are asking you to judge.** Whether the argument is clear and useful, whether the voice holds across 27 chapters, whether the named formulations earn their place, and whether the book is too long for what it carries. We would also like your independent view on the ending (Chapters 26 and 27) and on how well the chapters about AI will age.

## 2. Review form (five questions)

Please answer in a few sentences each. Cite chapter numbers where you can. Reading the whole book is welcome but not required; if you read part of it, say which part.

1. **Usefulness.** Name one idea from the book you would use in your own work within a month. Which chapter did it come from, and what would you do with it? If nothing, say so.
2. **Clarity.** Which chapter or passage lost you or felt padded? Which was the clearest? Please give chapter numbers.
3. **The named formulations.** Pick the two you found most useful and the one you found least convincing. For the last, say whether the idea is wrong, unclear, or already available elsewhere.
4. **Length and order.** If you had to cut one chapter or merge two, which and why? Would the book be better at a shorter length, or does each chapter carry something the others do not?
5. **Claims and credibility.** Did you find any claim that seemed wrong, overstated, or unsupported? Does the book make clear where it is relying on research and where it is giving the author's own view?

*Optional:* anything you would tell a friend about this book, and anything you would tell the author privately.

## 3. Recommended reviewers (five)

Reviewers are described by profile, not by name. The author or publisher should choose the individuals, since fit and availability cannot be checked from here.

| # | Role | Profile | Why this reviewer | Ask them to focus on |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Target reader | A working software or ML engineer, five to ten years in, who reads technical books for work and is not an AI specialist | The book's primary audience and the voice's natural fit | Questions 1 and 2; the Beginner chapters in Parts I–III |
| 2 | Target reader | A data analyst, product manager or researcher who makes decisions from evidence in a team setting | Tests whether the book travels beyond engineering | Chapters 11, 17, 18 and 25; Question 4 |
| 3 | Target reader | A non-technical knowledge worker such as an editor, lawyer or teacher, who is curious about AI but works mostly without it | Tests whether the AI-engineer register is accessible, and whether the personal stories land | Chapters 1–6; the Beginner flag; Question 2 |
| 4 | Subject-matter expert | A researcher in cognitive science, epistemology or the learning sciences who can judge how the book handles Kahneman, Polanyi, Popper, Clark, Chalmers and Ericsson | Checks that the synthesis is fair and the evidence note is honest | Chapters 5, 6, 16, 19, 24 and 26; the evidence note; Question 5 |
| 5 | Editor | A developmental editor with trade non-fiction experience in technology or ideas | Judges length, order, repetition and the strength of the ending | Question 4; the Part II and Part IV overlap; Chapters 26 and 27 |

**Selection notes.** Do not choose reviewers who know the author well, since they will find it hard to tell you a chapter should go. Ask reviewers 1 to 3 to read the front matter and four chapters of their choice, and reviewers 4 and 5 to read the whole book. Ask all five for answers within the same window so the responses can be compared.
