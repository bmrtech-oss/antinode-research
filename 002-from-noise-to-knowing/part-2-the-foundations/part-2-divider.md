## The Foundations, in Three Chapters

Part I described the problem. Part II describes the materials.

Before we can build a pipeline for converting information into knowledge, we need to be precise about three things: what information actually is, what knowledge actually is, and how a mind—any mind—actually builds it.

The three chapters that follow establish those foundations:

**Chapter 4 — The Raw Material** examines what information actually is: not a clean substance, but a mixture of signal and noise. It draws on Shannon's information theory, the anatomy of facts, observations, claims, models, and opinions, and the discipline of distinguishing what matters from what does not. It ends with a lesson from a retrieval system that failed because it could find information but could not tell signal from noise.

**Chapter 5 — The Target** defines what knowledge actually is: not a collection of facts, but a predictive, explanatory, coherent, and actionable model of reality. It draws on the knowledge loop, the anatomy of a model, the three kinds of knowing (that, how, and why), the distinction between explicit and tacit knowledge, and the four tests by which knowledge can be evaluated. It ends with a lesson from a benchmark that measured matching but not knowing.

**Chapter 6 — The Knower** examines how brains actually build knowledge: not by training on data, but by predicting, correcting, consolidating, pruning, and practicing. It draws on predictive processing, concept formation, memory architecture, synaptic homeostasis, dopamine, and deliberate practice. It ends with a lesson from a classifier that scored well on a test set and failed in production.

By the end of this part, you should have a precise understanding of the materials the pipeline works with. You should also see that the brain is the original knowledge engineer—that the pipeline is a deliberate, systematic version of what the brain does automatically.
