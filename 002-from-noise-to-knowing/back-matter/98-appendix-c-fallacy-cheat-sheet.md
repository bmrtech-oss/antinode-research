# Appendix C — Fallacy Cheat Sheet

---

## How to Use This Appendix

This is a **field guide**, not a textbook. It is designed to be scanned, not read. When you suspect an argument is flawed but cannot say why, find the pattern here, name it, and apply the antidote.

Chapter 17 explains the logic in depth. This appendix gives you the quick reference.

**The core insight:** every fallacy is a **counterfeit of valid reasoning**. It looks like an argument. It sounds like an argument. It may even persuade like an argument. But the support it offers is illusory. Your job is not to win the debate—it is to avoid being fooled.

---

## The Six-Step Detection Method

Run this on any argument that matters.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  1. IDENTIFY THE CONCLUSION                                     │
│     What is the claim being made?                               │
│                                                                 │
│  2. IDENTIFY THE PREMISES                                       │
│     What evidence or assumptions are offered in support?        │
│                                                                 │
│  3. CHECK THE LOGIC                                             │
│     Does the conclusion actually follow from the premises?      │
│                                                                 │
│  4. CHECK THE TERMS                                             │
│     Are the key words used clearly and consistently?            │
│                                                                 │
│  5. CHECK THE EVIDENCE                                          │
│     Is it relevant, sufficient, and representative?             │
│                                                                 │
│  6. CHECK THE RELEVANCE                                         │
│     Does the argument appeal to something irrelevant            │
│     (authority, emotion, popularity, ignorance)?                │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## Category 1 — Fallacies of Premise

*The starting point is broken.*

### 1. Begging the Question (*Petitio Principii*)

**What it is:** Assuming the truth of the conclusion in the premise.

**Example:** "Our benchmark is trustworthy because the best model scores highest on it, and we know it is the best model because it scores highest on our benchmark."

**How to spot it:** Ask: *Would I have to already accept the conclusion in order to accept the premise?* If yes, the argument is circular.

**Antidote:** Demand independent evidence. The premise must be established without relying on the conclusion.

---

### 2. False Premise

**What it is:** An argument that rests on a premise that is simply false.

**Example:** "All mammals can fly. Bats are mammals. Therefore, bats can fly." The conclusion is true—but for different reasons, not because of this argument.

**How to spot it:** Check each premise against evidence. A valid argument with a false premise can produce a true conclusion by accident, but it establishes nothing.

**Antidote:** Verify premises before evaluating the logic. Garbage in, garbage out.

---

### 3. Unwarranted Assumption

**What it is:** Assuming something that has not been proven and may not be true.

**Example:** "We should not legalize marijuana because it will lead to legalizing all drugs." (The assumption—the slippery slope—is unproven.)

**How to spot it:** Name the hidden assumptions. Ask what evidence supports them.

**Antidote:** Make assumptions explicit and demand evidence for them.

---

## Category 2 — Fallacies of Conclusion

*The premises may be fine, but the conclusion does not follow.*

### 4. Non Sequitur

**What it is:** A conclusion that does not follow from the premises. Latin for "it does not follow."

**Example:** "She is a good musician, therefore she will be a good teacher."

**How to spot it:** Ask: *What is the logical connection between premise and conclusion?*

**Antidote:** Require the missing premise to be stated—and then evaluated.

---

### 5. Hasty Generalization

**What it is:** Drawing a general conclusion from a small or unrepresentative sample.

**Example:** "I met two rude people from New York. Therefore, people from New York are rude."

**How to spot it:** Check the sample size and representativeness. Would a larger sample likely change the result?

**Antidote:** Demand larger, more representative samples—or downgrade the claim from "all" to "some."

---

### 6. False Dilemma (*False Dichotomy*)

**What it is:** Presenting a situation as if there are only two options, when there are more.

**Example:** "Either we ship the model this sprint, or we admit the project has failed."

**How to spot it:** Ask: *Are there other options being excluded?* Almost always, yes.

**Antidote:** Name the excluded middle. "Neutral," "conditional support," "critical support," and "opposed to the tactics but not the goal" are all real positions.

---

### 7. Straw Man

**What it is:** Misrepresenting someone's argument to make it easier to attack.

**Example:** "My opponent wants to reduce military spending. He wants to leave our country defenseless."

**How to spot it:** Ask: *Is this a fair representation of the strongest version of their position?*

**Antidote:** Steelman the opponent. Restate their argument in its strongest form before responding.

---

### 8. Slippery Slope

**What it is:** Claiming that one small step will inevitably lead to an extreme outcome, without establishing the chain.

**Example:** "If we allow students to redo tests, soon they will expect to redo everything, and eventually no one will accept responsibility for anything."

**How to spot it:** Ask: *Is each link in the chain actually established?* Usually only the first step is plausible.

**Antidote:** Demand evidence for each link. Identify where the chain breaks.

---

## Category 3 — Fallacies of Ambiguity

*The words shift meaning.*

### 9. Equivocation

**What it is:** Using the same word in different senses within one argument.

**Example:** "Every model has a bias term. Bias is unfair. Therefore every model is unfair." (*Bias* means a statistical offset in one sentence and prejudice in the next.)

**How to spot it:** Track key terms. Do they mean the same thing each time?

**Antidote:** Define terms precisely and hold them fixed. If the meaning shifts, the argument collapses.

---

### 10. Amphiboly

**What it is:** Ambiguity arising from grammatical structure.

**Example:** "Fixed the bug in the parser with the new tokenizer." (Was the bug fixed using the tokenizer, or is the bug in the parser that has the tokenizer?)

**How to spot it:** Parse the sentence more than one way. If both readings are possible and the conclusion depends on one, suspect amphiboly.

**Antidote:** Rewrite the sentence to eliminate the ambiguity.

---

### 11. Accent

**What it is:** Ambiguity arising from emphasis.

**Example:** "I never SAID the results were wrong." (Stressed that way, it implies I suggested it some other way.)

**How to spot it:** Change the emphasis. Does the meaning change?

**Antidote:** Ask what is being emphasized and what is being smuggled in by that emphasis.

---

## Category 4 — Causal Fallacies

*The reasoning about cause and effect is broken.*

### 12. Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc

**What it is:** Assuming that because B followed A, A caused B.

**Example:** "I wore my lucky socks, and my team won. Therefore, my socks caused the win."

**How to spot it:** Ask: *Is there a mechanism? Or just temporal sequence?*

**Antidote:** Distinguish succession from causation. Look for a mechanism and control for confounders.

---

### 13. Cum Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc

**What it is:** Assuming that because A and B occur together, one caused the other.

**Example:** "People who eat breakfast are healthier. Therefore, breakfast causes health."

**How to spot it:** Ask: *Is this correlation or causation? Could a third factor explain both?*

**Antidote:** Demand causal evidence—experiments, natural experiments, or strong observational designs.

---

### 14. Ignoring a Common Cause

**What it is:** Assuming two correlated events are causally related when both are caused by a third factor.

**Example:** "Servers with more alerts have more outages. Therefore alerts cause outages." (The common cause is load and complexity.)

**How to spot it:** Ask: *What third variable could explain both?*

**Antidote:** Search for confounders before accepting a causal claim.

---

### 15. Reverse Causation

**What it is:** Assuming A causes B when actually B causes A.

**Example:** "Depressed people watch more TV. Therefore, TV causes depression." (Or: depression causes TV watching.)

**How to spot it:** Ask: *Which direction is more plausible? Could it be bidirectional?*

**Antidote:** Use temporal data and causal designs to establish direction.

---

## Category 5 — Statistical Fallacies

*The numbers are being misused.*

### 16. Base Rate Neglect

**What it is:** Ignoring the prior probability when evaluating a specific case.

**Example:** "The test is 99% accurate, and I tested positive—so there's a 99% chance I have the disease." (If the disease affects 1 in 10,000, the real probability is around 1%.)

**How to spot it:** Ask: *What is the base rate?* Before interpreting a test result, know how common the condition is.

**Antidote:** Use Bayesian reasoning. Combine base rates with test accuracy.

---

### 17. Survivorship Bias

**What it is:** Drawing conclusions only from the cases that survived, while ignoring the failures.

**Example:** "All successful companies had charismatic leaders. Therefore, charisma causes success." (Ignores charismatic leaders whose companies failed.)

**How to spot it:** Ask: *Where are the failures? What is missing from the sample?*

**Antidote:** Actively seek the full sample—including the failures that are no longer visible.

---

### 18. Regression to the Mean

**What it is:** Mistaking a statistical artifact for a causal effect. Extreme values tend to be followed by less extreme ones.

**Example:** A student scores 99, then 85. The teacher concludes the student "got lazy." (The drop is partly chance.)

**How to spot it:** Ask: *Is this an extreme value? Is a return toward average expected?*

**Antidote:** Use control groups. Do not attribute regression to intervention.

---

### 19. Cherry-Picking

**What it is:** Selecting only the evidence that supports the conclusion, while ignoring the rest.

**Example:** "Studies show coffee is good for you." (Citing only the favorable studies.)

**How to spot it:** Ask: *What is being left out? Is there a systematic review?*

**Antidote:** Demand the full body of evidence—preferably a systematic review or meta-analysis.

---

## Category 6 — Fallacies of Relevance

*The argument appeals to something irrelevant to the conclusion.*

### 20. Ad Hominem

**What it is:** Attacking the person rather than the argument.

**Example:** "You can't trust his economic policy—he's been divorced twice."

**How to spot it:** Ask: *Is this about the claim or the claimant?*

**Antidote:** Separate the argument from the arguer. A bad person can make a good argument.

---

### 21. Appeal to Authority

**What it is:** Accepting a claim because an authority figure said it, rather than because of the evidence.

**Example:** "This diet must work—a famous actor endorsed it."

**How to spot it:** Ask: *Is the authority relevant? Is there independent evidence?*

**Antidote:** Require expertise in the relevant domain and evidence independent of the authority.

---

### 22. Appeal to Emotion

**What it is:** Using emotion—fear, pity, anger, enthusiasm—in place of evidence.

**Example:** "Think of the engineers who will lose their jobs if we fall behind. We must adopt this tool today."

**How to spot it:** Ask: *Is the emotion doing the work that evidence should do?*

**Antidote:** Separate the emotional force of a claim from its evidential support.

---

### 23. Appeal to Ignorance (*Argumentum ad Ignorantiam*)

**What it is:** Claiming something is true because it has not been proven false—or false because it has not been proven true.

**Example:** "No one has proven that aliens don't exist. Therefore, they do."

**How to spot it:** Ask: *Who holds the burden of proof?*

**Antidote:** The burden of proof lies with the person making the positive claim. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

---

### 24. Bandwagon Fallacy

**What it is:** Claiming something is true or good because many people believe it.

**Example:** "Everyone is investing in this stock. It must be a good investment."

**How to spot it:** Ask: *Is popularity being offered as proof?*

**Antidote:** Popularity is not evidence of truth. Crowds can be wrong.

---

### 25. Appeal to Tradition

**What it is:** Claiming something is true or good because it is old or traditional.

**Example:** "This has been our process for fifty years. It must be right."

**How to spot it:** Ask: *Is age being offered as proof?*

**Antidote:** Tradition is evidence of survival, not of correctness. Circumstances change.

---

### 26. Tu Quoque ("You Too")

**What it is:** Deflecting criticism by accusing the critic of the same fault.

**Example:** "You say I should exercise more, but you don't exercise either."

**How to spot it:** Ask: *Does the accuser's hypocrisy affect the truth of the claim?*

**Antidote:** Separate the truth of the claim from the consistency of the person making it.

---

### 27. Burden of Proof Shifting

**What it is:** Demanding that the other side disprove your claim, rather than proving it yourself.

**Example:** "Prove that my theory is wrong." (The burden is on the person making the claim.)

**How to spot it:** Ask: *Who is making the positive claim? Who should be proving what?*

**Antidote:** Insist on the correct allocation of the burden of proof.

---

## AI-Specific Fallacies

Language models introduce a new set of failure modes. These are not logical fallacies in the classical sense—they are **computational artifacts** that can be mistaken for reasoning.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  AI-SPECIFIC FAILURE MODES                                      │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                 │
│  1. HALLUCINATION                                               │
│     Confident fabrication. The model states false things        │
│     with the same fluency as true ones.                         │
│     DETECT: Check every factual claim against a real source.    │
│                                                                 │
│  2. FABRICATED CITATIONS                                        │
│     Invented sources that look real—plausible authors,          │
│     plausible titles, plausible journals.                       │
│     DETECT: Verify that every cited source actually exists      │
│     and actually says what is claimed.                          │
│                                                                 │
│  3. SYCOPHANCY                                                  │
│     Agreeing with the user rather than correcting them.         │
│     The model tells you what you want to hear.                  │
│     DETECT: Ask it to argue the opposite. Ask it to critique    │
│     your position. If it flips too easily, it was flattering.   │
│                                                                 │
│  4. FALSE PRECISION                                             │
│     Confident numbers and specifics where the model has         │
│     no basis for precision.                                     │
│     DETECT: Ask for the basis of every number.                  │
│                                                                 │
│  5. PLAUSIBLE NON SEQUITUR                                      │
│     Text that reads like reasoning but contains no valid        │
│     inference. Fluent, confident, and empty.                    │
│     DETECT: Run the six-step detection method manually.         │
│                                                                 │
│  6. STALE AUTHORITY                                             │
│     Citing older sources as current, or presenting              │
│     outdated consensus as present fact.                         │
│     DETECT: Check dates. Ask what has changed recently.         │
│                                                                 │
│  7. CONSENSUS CONFABULATION                                     │
│     Asserting that "experts agree" without evidence,            │
│     or inventing a consensus that does not exist.               │
│     DETECT: Ask which experts, which studies, which sources.    │
│                                                                 │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

<!-- diagram-alt -->*Diagram description: A reference list names seven AI failure modes: hallucination, fabricated citations, sycophancy, false precision, plausible non sequitur, stale authority, and consensus confabulation. Each is paired with a brief description and a way to detect it.*


**The rule for AI:** *Fluent is not the same as correct. Confident is not the same as true. Always verify.*

## A Final Warning

Learning the names of fallacies is easy. Resisting them is hard.

The reason is not intellectual—it is psychological. Fallacies are **persuasive** precisely because they exploit our biases. The confirmation bias makes us want to accept arguments that support our beliefs. The appeal to emotion makes us feel the force of a claim before we evaluate it. The ad hominem gives us a socially acceptable reason to dismiss what we would rather not hear.

Knowing the names will not save you. **The discipline of testing will.**

When you feel an argument pulling you—when it feels compelling, satisfying, or obviously right—that is the moment to slow down. Ask the six questions. Run the detection method. Look for the flaw.

The fallacy is the enemy without. But it can only defeat you if you do not check your own defenses.
